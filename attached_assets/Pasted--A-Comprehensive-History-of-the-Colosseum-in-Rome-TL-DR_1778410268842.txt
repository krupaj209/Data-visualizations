# A Comprehensive History of the Colosseum in Rome

## TL;DR
- The Colosseum (formally the Flavian Amphitheatre) was built in central Rome between AD 72 and 80 by the Flavian emperors Vespasian, Titus, and Domitian as a populist gift to the Roman people, financed largely by the spoils of the Sack of Jerusalem (AD 70) and built in part by enslaved Jewish prisoners of war.
- For roughly four centuries it was the largest and most technologically sophisticated entertainment venue in the ancient world — hosting gladiatorial combats, animal hunts (venationes), public executions, mythological reenactments, and possibly mock naval battles — before falling into disuse after gladiatorial games ended in the early 5th century and animal hunts ceased in 523.
- After more than a millennium of earthquakes, neglect, and use as a quarry, fortress, cemetery, and Christian shrine, the Colosseum was rescued by 18th–21st century papal and Italian conservation efforts; today, as a UNESCO World Heritage Site (1980) and a New Seven Wonder of the World (2007), it draws nearly 15 million visitors a year and serves as a global symbol both of Rome's imperial past and of the modern campaign against the death penalty.

## Key Findings
- **Founding purpose was political theater.** Vespasian, founder of the Flavian dynasty after the chaotic "Year of the Four Emperors" (AD 69), commissioned the Colosseum on the drained artificial lake of Nero's hated Domus Aurea — explicitly returning to the people land Nero had appropriated. It was the first amphitheatre placed at the symbolic and geographic heart of Rome.
- **Funding came from Jerusalem.** A reconstructed dedicatory inscription found in 1813 records that Vespasian ordered the new amphitheatre built "from the proceeds of the booty" — i.e., the treasure looted from the Temple of Jerusalem in AD 70. Jewish prisoners likely supplied much of the unskilled labor, alongside Roman engineers and craftsmen.
- **Construction took roughly 8–10 years for the main structure (72–80 AD), and ~24 years overall.** Vespasian completed three stories before his death in 79; Titus finished and dedicated it in AD 80; Domitian (r. 81–96) added the underground hypogeum and a top gallery, completing the building as we essentially know it.
- **It was an engineering marvel.** Elliptical (188 × 156 m, ~48–50 m tall, ~6-acre footprint), freestanding (unlike most amphitheatres, which leaned on hillsides), and built from ~100,000 cubic meters of travertine plus tuff, brick, and Roman concrete, held together by some 300 tonnes of iron clamps. It seated an estimated 50,000–80,000 spectators (averaging ~65,000), with 80 numbered entrances allowing rapid ingress and egress. A retractable awning (the velarium) operated by sailors from the imperial fleet at Misenum shaded much of the seating.
- **The inaugural games of AD 80 lasted 100 days.** Titus, facing the trauma of Vesuvius (79), a fire, and a plague, opened the building with games described by Martial in his *Liber de Spectaculis*. Cassius Dio reports more than 9,000 wild animals were killed; combats included gladiator pairs, women gladiators, mythological executions (e.g., a criminal "Laureolus" killed by a bear in a reenactment of Prometheus), and at least one naumachia (mock naval battle).
- **Spectacles followed a standard format**: morning *venationes* (animal hunts featuring lions, leopards, bears, elephants, rhinos, hippos, crocodiles, ostriches, even giraffes, sourced across the empire); midday *executions ad bestias* of criminals and prisoners of war; afternoon gladiatorial combat. Trajan reportedly celebrated his Dacian victory in AD 107 with 123 days of games involving 11,000 animals and 10,000 gladiators.
- **The Colosseum suffered repeatedly from disasters.** A lightning-induced fire in AD 217 destroyed the wooden upper interior, closing the building until repairs by Macrinus, Elagabalus, and Alexander Severus (completed under Gordian III by ~240); further damage came from lightning (250s), earthquakes (AD 443, 484/508, 847, 1231, 1349, 1703), and progressive looting.
- **Its active life ended in stages.** Christian emperors progressively curtailed the games: Constantine restricted them, Honorius issued bans in 399 and 404 (the last gladiatorial combat is traditionally dated to 1 January 404, after the monk Telemachus was killed trying to stop a fight, per the 5th-century historian Theodoret). Animal hunts continued until AD 523, the last recorded games, held under the Ostrogothic king Theodoric.
- **For a thousand years it was Rome's quarry and fortress.** From the 6th century the arena was used as a cemetery and a chapel was inserted; vaulted spaces became housing and workshops. Around 1200 the Frangipane family fortified it as a castle. The catastrophic earthquake of 9 September 1349 collapsed the southern outer ring; the rubble pile (the *coxa Colisei*, "thigh of the Colosseum") was quarried until the 18th century. Stone from the Colosseum was used for St. Peter's Basilica, the Palazzo Venezia, the Palazzo Farnese, the Palazzo della Cancelleria, the Ponte Sisto, and many other Roman buildings — in 1452 alone, 2,500 cartloads were carted away. Iron clamps were systematically extracted, leaving the pockmarks visible today.
- **The Catholic Church saved the structure.** Although the tradition of Christian martyrdom in the Colosseum is not historically documented (the Catholic Encyclopedia itself concedes there are no historical grounds for it), this *belief* preserved the building. In 1749, Pope Benedict XIV, prompted by St. Leonard of Port Maurice, forbade further quarrying and (in 1750/1756) consecrated the Colosseum to the Passion of Christ and the early martyrs, erecting Stations of the Cross within. Successive popes (Pius VII, Leo XII, Gregory XVI, Pius IX) funded buttressing and restoration.
- **Modern restoration is ongoing and hybrid.** Italian state restoration began in earnest after unification (the Stations were removed in 1874); Mussolini excavated the hypogeum in the 1930s and used the monument as fascist backdrop, building the Via dei Fori Imperiali (1932) on its axis. A €25 million private restoration funded by Diego Della Valle of Tod's (2013–2021) cleaned the façades and the hypogea; in 2021 the Italian government allocated €18.5 million to reconstruct a retractable wooden arena floor (Accoya wood, designed by Milan Ingegneria), originally targeted for 2023.
- **The Colosseum is now Italy's most-visited cultural site.** In 2024 the Colosseum Archaeological Park (Colosseum, Roman Forum, Palatine, Domus Aurea) recorded 14,733,395 visitors and €101.9 million in revenue — almost 20% more visitors and 28% more revenue than 2023, and far more than the Louvre's 2024 attendance.

## Details

### I. Origins and Construction (AD 70–96)

The site of the Colosseum was originally a residential valley between the Caelian, Esquiline, and Palatine hills. After the Great Fire of AD 64, Nero seized the area for his vast palace complex, the Domus Aurea, centered on an artificial lake and a colossal bronze statue of himself, the Colossus of Nero. When Nero's misrule ended in suicide in 68, four emperors (Galba, Otho, Vitellius, Vespasian) contested the throne in a single year. Vespasian — a soldier-emperor of relatively humble origin — emerged victorious and embarked on a building program designed to erase Nero's mark, restore Senate authority, and signal *Roma resurgens* ("Rome rising again").

Planning began in AD 70 and construction in 72 (some sources say 73–75). The lake was drained, concrete foundations up to 6 meters deep were poured to guard against the unstable alluvial soil, and a 7-hectare imperial complex of amphitheatre, gladiatorial schools (the *Ludus Magnus*, *Ludus Dacicus*, *Ludus Gallicus*, *Ludus Matutinus*), the Baths of Titus, and the Sanctuary of Claudius rose from Nero's ruins. The reconstructed dedicatory inscription — "I[mp(erator)] Caes(ar) Vespasi[anus Aug(ustus)] amphitheatru[m novum] [ex] manub(i)s [fieri iussit]" — confirms the funding source: the *manubiae*, or general's share of war booty, from the Sack of Jerusalem in AD 70 by Vespasian's son Titus.

Vespasian died in 79 with the Colosseum complete only to the third storey. Titus finished the fourth (and originally the wooden top tier) and dedicated the building in AD 80 with 100 days of games. Domitian (r. 81–96) added the *hypogeum* — a two-level underground network of 80 vertical shafts, 28 capstan-operated wooden elevators (each capable of lifting ~300 kg in roughly seven seconds, powered by eight men), corridors, animal cages, and storage rooms — and raised the seating with a high gallery for women, the poor, and slaves, bringing capacity to its peak.

The name "Colosseum" is medieval, first attested by St. Bede around AD 730 and in popular usage in Rome from the 11th century. It almost certainly derives from the *Colossus of Nero*, the giant bronze statue (later refashioned into a sun god) that stood beside the building. In antiquity Romans called it simply *amphitheatrum* or, occasionally, *Amphitheatrum Caesareum*; "Flavian Amphitheatre" is a modern coinage by classicists.

### II. Architecture and Engineering

The Colosseum is an ellipse with a major axis of 188 m and minor axis of 156 m, rising in four stories to ~48–50 m. The outer façade is travertine quarried at Tivoli (about 100,000 m³, ~292,000 tonnes), with internal walls of tuff, brick, and *opus caementicium* (Roman concrete). Three superimposed arcaded stories use Tuscan/Doric, Ionic, and Corinthian half-columns respectively; the fourth, attic-level wall is a solid surface pierced by small windows and 240 corbels that anchored the masts of the *velarium*, a vast retractable canvas awning manipulated by sailors from the Misenum fleet — the open central oculus left the arena itself unshaded.

The arena floor — 83 × 48 m, wooden planking covered with sand (Latin *harena*) — concealed Domitian's hypogeum, whose mechanical complexity, in the words of the German archaeologist Heinz-Jürgen Beste, exceeded anything until the Industrial Revolution. Eighty entrances on the ground floor — 76 of them public — and an internal *vomitorium* circulation system allowed up to 80,000 spectators to enter and exit with ease. Seating was strictly hierarchical: the emperor and Vestal Virgins in the imperial box, senators on the lowest tier, equites above them, ordinary citizens above that, and women, slaves, and the poor in the wooden uppermost gallery.

### III. The Games and Their Audience

For four centuries the Colosseum was the largest and most prestigious venue for *munera* (gladiatorial games) and *venationes* (beast hunts) in the Roman world. A typical day followed a fixed pattern: morning animal hunts with skilled *venatores*; midday executions of criminals (*damnatio ad bestias*) and prisoners of war, sometimes choreographed as mythological reenactments (Martial describes a condemned man playing Prometheus to be killed by a bear, and another playing Daedalus); and afternoon gladiatorial combat, the headline event.

Gladiators included condemned criminals, prisoners of war, slaves trained in the schools, and a smaller number of free volunteers (*auctorati*) seeking fame or money. Most fights were not to the death — gladiators were expensive investments — and a wounded man could appeal to the emperor and crowd for *missio* (release). The most famous gladiator at the inaugural games, Carpophorus, was celebrated by Martial for slaying numerous beasts.

The animal hunts demonstrated Rome's imperial reach: lions and panthers from North Africa and Syria, tigers from Persia, hippos and crocodiles from Egypt, giraffes, ostriches, rhinos, and elephants from sub-Saharan Africa, bears from northern Europe. Trajan's Dacian games of AD 107 reportedly killed 11,000 animals over 123 days. Some scholars estimate up to 400,000 people and approximately one million animals died in the Colosseum across its operational life — figures that should be treated as broad estimates rather than precise tallies.

**Naumachiae (mock naval battles)** are reported in the Colosseum during Titus's inaugural games (Cassius Dio, Suetonius, Martial all attest to it) and possibly under Domitian around 85 CE, before the hypogeum was completed and made flooding impossible. Modern archaeologists (notably Beste and the engineer Martin Crapper) remain divided on the logistics, but the consensus holds that limited-scale naumachiae were briefly possible at the building's beginning.

The popular tradition that early Christians were martyred in the Colosseum has no contemporary documentary support. The 5th-century historian Theodoret records that on 1 January 404 the monk Telemachus (or Almachus) was killed in the arena while trying to stop a gladiatorial fight; the emperor Honorius is said to have used his death to enforce the abolition of gladiatorial games. The story is genuine in outline but Telemachus is not a Colosseum-specific Christian martyr in the popular sense.

### IV. Decline (3rd–6th centuries)

In AD 217, during the Vulcanalia festival, lightning struck the Colosseum and destroyed the wooden upper interior. Macrinus began rebuilding; Elagabalus and Alexander Severus continued the work, which was not formally completed until coins celebrating the restoration were issued under Gordian III around 240. Further repairs followed lightning damage under Decius (~250) and additional work in 320. Earthquakes in 443 and 484/508 caused renewed damage; senatorial prefects (Iunius Valerius Bellicius, Rufius Cecina Felix Lampadius, Basilius) financed restorations, sometimes inscribing repairs onto reused marble seats.

The decline of the games tracked the rise of Christianity and the financial exhaustion of the late Empire. Constantine restricted the games; Theodosius I (who made Christianity the state religion) issued edicts against them. Honorius banned gladiatorial combat in 399 and again in 404 — the last firmly attested gladiatorial fight. Animal hunts continued until 523, organized for the consulship of Anicius Maximus by Eutharic, son-in-law of the Ostrogothic king Theodoric. After that, the building's official life as an arena ended.

### V. The Long Middle Ages (6th–14th centuries): Quarry, Fortress, Shrine

By the late 6th century a small chapel had been inserted into the structure, the arena floor became a cemetery, and the vaulted spaces beneath the seats were rented out as housing and workshops — records show this continued into the 12th century. Around 1200 the powerful Frangipane family converted the Colosseum into a fortified palace; the Annibaldi family later did the same. In 1144 the Roman commune banished the baronial families.

The medieval Colosseum was *not* venerated as a Christian shrine: it appears in no pilgrim itinerary or in the 12th-century *Mirabilia Urbis Romae*. Instead it was, in modern terms, a quarry. Earthquakes — especially the catastrophic event of 9 September 1349 (estimated magnitude 6.7–7.0), which Petrarch described in a letter — collapsed the southern outer ring. The mass of fallen travertine, called the *coxa Colisei*, was hauled away for centuries; in 1452 alone, 2,500 cartloads were extracted. Marble was burned in lime kilns; iron clamps were pried from the walls (an estimated 300 tonnes recovered), leaving the pockmarks still visible. Colosseum stone went into St. Peter's Basilica, the Palazzo Venezia, the Palazzo Farnese, the Palazzo della Cancelleria, the Ponte Sisto, the Scala Santa, and dozens of other Roman buildings.

### VI. From Renaissance to Risorgimento (15th–19th centuries)

In 1377, after the Avignon Papacy, the religious order *Arciconfraternita del SS. Salvatore ad Sancta Sanctorum* moved into the northern portion of the building and remained until the early 19th century. Pope Eugenius IV and successive Renaissance popes intervened against further demolition — partly out of patriotism, partly because the conviction grew (from the late 16th century onwards, originating with St. Pius V and codified by writers like Martinelli in 1653) that the soil of the arena was sacred with martyrs' blood. Pope Sixtus V (1585–1590) had floated turning it into a wool factory employing prostitutes; in 1671 Cardinal Altieri authorized bullfights inside it, prompting public outcry. In 1675, Pope Clement X closed the exterior arcades, sanctifying the site.

In 1749/1756 Pope Benedict XIV, urged by St. Leonard of Port Maurice, forbade further quarrying and dedicated the Colosseum to the Passion of Christ and the martyrs, installing the Stations of the Cross around the arena (they remained until 1874). Pius VII (1805), Leo XII (1825), Gregory XVI (1845), and Pius IX (1852) all funded major restorations; Raffaele Stern's brick spur buttress on the eastern side (1807) and Giuseppe Valadier's western buttress (1826) saved the surviving outer ring from total collapse. The 19th century also brought the first scientific archaeology: Carlo Fea's cleaning campaign (1804), Antonio Nibby's drainage works (1828), Luis Joseph Duc's first modern survey (1830), and progressive excavation of the hypogeum.

The Colosseum became the centerpiece of the European Grand Tour. Romantic-era artists and writers — most famously Lord Byron in Canto IV of *Childe Harold's Pilgrimage* (1818), describing the arena viewed by moonlight ("I see before me the Gladiator lie") — turned the ruin into a global symbol of melancholy grandeur and *sic transit gloria mundi*. Painters like Thomas Cole rendered its overgrown interior; by 1855, naturalists had catalogued 420 plant species growing on its walls.

### VII. Modern Italy and Fascist Romanità (1870–1945)

After the unification of Italy (1861) and the seizure of Rome (1870), the Italian state took over conservation. The Stations of the Cross were removed in 1874 over Catholic protest; the arena was extensively excavated, exposing the hypogeum; gas pipes and drainage were installed; in 1895, 89 burial places dating to AD 4th–6th centuries were discovered.

Under Mussolini (1922–43), the Colosseum became a backdrop for fascist ideology. The dictator's regime fully excavated the hypogeum and laid out the Via dei Fori Imperiali (originally Via dell'Impero), inaugurated 9 April 1932 by Mussolini on horseback, which involved demolishing 138 buildings to drive a 1-km parade route from the Piazza Venezia to the Colosseum. The boulevard symbolically linked Mussolini's headquarters in Palazzo Venezia with the imperial monuments, embodying his doctrine of *romanità* ("Roman-ness"). The destruction was indiscriminate: medieval and Renaissance neighborhoods, the Velia hill, churches, and uncatalogued archaeological finds were lost. The Allied Powers deliberately spared the Colosseum during World War II.

### VIII. The Colosseum Today (1945–2026)

In 1980 UNESCO inscribed the Historic Centre of Rome — including the Colosseum — as a World Heritage Site. In 2007 it was named one of the New Seven Wonders of the World by global popular vote. It appears on the Italian €0.05 coin.

A major state-funded restoration ran 1993–2000 and again from 1995, raising visitor access from ~15% of the building to a much larger share. In 2011, Diego Della Valle, head of Tod's, pledged €25 million for the first full cleaning and structural repair in the Colosseum's history; phase one (façade cleaning of ~13,300 m² and replacement of arch enclosures) was completed on 1 July 2016, and phase two (the hypogea, with a 160-meter walkway opening previously inaccessible areas) was completed in 2021. The public–private nature of the deal was politically controversial in Italy.

In December 2020 / May 2021 the Italian Ministry of Culture, then under Dario Franceschini, allocated €18.5 million for a retractable arena floor of Accoya-wood panels (a chemically modified pine); the design competition was won by an engineering team led by Milan Ingegneria with the architecture firm Labics, with Beste as archaeological consultant. The original target was 2023; works are ongoing as of 2026.

The Colosseum has acquired a new symbolic role: since 2000, in coordination with the Community of Sant'Egidio and Amnesty International, the city of Rome illuminates it in gold for 48 hours every time a death sentence is commuted, an execution is halted, or a jurisdiction abolishes capital punishment — a deliberate inversion of its original use. Each Good Friday the pope leads the Way of the Cross at the Colosseum, a tradition revived by John XXIII and made permanent under Paul VI; on 3 April 2026, Pope Leo XIV personally carried the cross through all 14 stations, the first pope to do so since the early years of John Paul II.

In 2024 the Colosseum Archaeological Park welcomed 14,733,395 visitors and recorded €101.9 million in receipts — a 20% increase in attendance and 28% in revenue over 2023, making it the most visited cultural site in Italy and one of the most visited monuments on earth.

## Recommendations

For a reader pursuing this topic, the staged path is:

1. **Start with the standard scholarly synthesis.** Read the Wikipedia "Colosseum" article and Britannica's entry to anchor dates and dimensions, then move to Mark Cartwright's *World History Encyclopedia* essay and the official *Parco archeologico del Colosseo* (colosseo.it) site for current-state information.
2. **Go to the primary sources.** Martial's *Liber de Spectaculis* is the only contemporary literary record of the inaugural games; Suetonius's *Lives of the Caesars* and Cassius Dio's *Roman History* (Book LXVI) provide the principal narrative accounts. The dedicatory inscription discovered in 1813 is the firmest documentary anchor for funding by Jewish War spoils.
3. **Read modern monographs for depth.** Nathan T. Elkins, *A Monument to Dynasty and Death: The Story of Rome's Colosseum and the Emperors Who Built It* (2019), is the best recent academic synthesis. Heinz-Jürgen Beste's research at the German Archaeological Institute remains authoritative on the hypogeum's mechanics; Keith Hopkins and Mary Beard's *The Colosseum* (2005) is the best concise scholarly book.
4. **Visit strategically.** If touring in person, book a hypogeum + arena floor ticket (currently the most informative experience) and avoid mid-summer; spring and fall yield smaller crowds and more conservation activity to observe.
5. **Track one ongoing benchmark.** The completion of the Milan Ingegneria retractable arena floor (originally 2023, now slipped) will be the single most visible change to the Colosseum in two centuries; once installed, it will allow on-arena cultural events for the first time since the 6th century. Monitor *colosseo.it* and Italian Ministry of Culture announcements.

What would change these recommendations: significant new archaeological discoveries (especially around naumachia hydraulics or the hypogeum), a major earthquake or climate-related structural event, or the reopening of the upper tiers under future restoration phases would all justify revisiting the standard narrative summarized here.

## Caveats

- **Casualty and animal-kill numbers are estimates, not records.** Figures like "9,000 animals at the inaugural games," "over 400,000 humans and one million animals across the building's life," and "11,000 animals over 123 days under Trajan" come from ancient sources (Cassius Dio, Suetonius) that mixed fact, rumor, and propaganda; modern historians treat them as orders-of-magnitude indicators.
- **The Christian-martyr tradition is unsupported by contemporary evidence.** Despite Pope Benedict XIV's 1756 dedication, the Catholic Encyclopedia itself acknowledges no historical grounds for treating the Colosseum specifically as a martyrs' shrine; the building's medieval-era omission from pilgrim itineraries and its long use as a Frangipane fortress are themselves evidence against it. The tradition's *cultural* importance is real and saved the structure; its *historical* basis is not.
- **Naumachiae in the Colosseum remain debated.** Cassius Dio, Martial, and Suetonius all describe sea battles there during the inaugural games and possibly under Domitian, and 2007/2020 hydraulic-engineering and groundwater studies (by Crapper and by Cristina Di Salvo) suggest brief flooding was technically feasible. But the relatively shallow depths achievable, the lack of physical traces, and the construction of the hypogeum (which made flooding impossible from c. AD 85) mean the scale would have been modest. The dramatic flooded-Colosseum scene in *Gladiator II* (2024) is dramatized far beyond any evidence.
- **Telemachus's death is reported only by Theodoret** writing nearly half a century later (5th century); some details (the gladiator's spear, the year 404) come from later sources and may be embroidered. The trend toward abolishing the games is solidly attested, but the precise role of Telemachus is hagiographic.
- **Visitor and revenue figures vary by source.** Some sources cite 7 million annual visitors (pre-pandemic baseline), others 12 million, and the official 2024 Italian Ministry of Culture figure of 14.7 million reflects the entire Colosseum Archaeological Park (Colosseum + Roman Forum + Palatine + Domus Aurea on a single ticket), not the Colosseum alone — the figures are not directly comparable across years and definitions.
- **Construction details (exact start year, role of slave labor)** vary slightly between sources. The standard "started AD 72" is reasonable but some scholars argue 70 or 75; the proportion of Jewish slave labor versus paid Roman labor cannot be quantified precisely.
- **The "Domitian added a fifth tier" framing in some popular sources is misleading.** The building is generally described as four-storied; Domitian added a *high gallery* (sometimes called the *maenianum summum in ligneis*) at the top, which expanded seating but is not a fully separate fifth story.
